03

A living tradition

People were making music long before notation entered the chat. They listened, copied, answered, changed things, and passed them on.

Street-band music is a living tradition. So is banjo music. So is Christian choral music. These traditions are aural and oral: carried in bodies, memory, repetition, correction, and the small generosity of someone showing you the next part. A page can help. It is not where the music lives.

I care about technique. I like a band that is tight enough to change the weather. Musical literacy matters too—but it is bigger than reading notation. It is knowing how to enter, how to listen, how to hold a part, how to recover, and how to make everybody around you sound better.

* * *The radical thing is not lowering the bar. It is taking back the right to make the sound.

Professionalization can turn music from a natural human activity into a service supplied by experts. The stage gets taller. The audience gets quieter. Folk music insists on another arrangement: skill without a priesthood, mastery that circulates, traditions changed by the people carrying them.

And then there is the ecstatic part. Playing can pull you out of abstraction and into a collective body: breath, pavement, sweat, pulse, other people. A street band can do this without permission, electricity, or a room built for listening. It can turn a march into a dance and make the line between musician and crowd briefly unreliable.
